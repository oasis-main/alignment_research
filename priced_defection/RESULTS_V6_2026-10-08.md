# Pass 6 results — roles as choices, one shared budget (2026-10-08)

**Pre-registered predictions P1–P4 are in the module docstring of
`tournament6.py`, written before the first run.** Two of the four failed. One
produced the strongest result in the study so far. One cell exposed a
calibration failure in my own pricing that invalidates part of the sweep.
All four outcomes are below, including the failures.

Harness: `tournament6.py`, 30 seeds per cell, N=20, 200 rounds, mc=32, pure
stdlib, every run seeded. `roles="assigned"` delegates to `tournament5.run5`
and reproduces every pass-5 number seed-for-seed (`--selftest` PASS).

Raw: `raw/results_v6_{A..E}.json`, `raw/sweep_v6_{A..E}.txt`.

## The structural change

Passes 1–5 optimised exactly one thing: a pre-designated attacker's decision
to move. Pass 6 removes the attacker list. Every agent, every round, picks one
action from one menu — idle, grow, post a defensive posture, scout, seize,
prepare a takeover — and pays for it from one budget fed by both the ordinary
game's payoffs and income from capability itself.

Three freebies from pass 5 are now priced:

| Pass 5 | Pass 6 |
|---|---|
| Attackers are a fixed list | Nobody is designated; attacking is an observed behaviour |
| Everyone defends automatically, for free, without consent | A posture costs `c_def`; **only paid postures are counted in any response** |
| Defender catch-up growth `g_def` is free | Growth costs `c_grow` and competes with defence and attack |

Capability is priced endogenously: an agent values a unit of its own
capability at the income it yields forever plus its effect on its own takeover
odds, the latter by a 5% finite difference on its own Monte Carlo under common
random numbers. There is no free parameter for what capability is worth.

**Lost relative to pass 5:** coalitions. Locking arms is a choice and we have
no bargaining model for who joins or how spoils split, so rather than keep an
assigned coalition we removed it. Pass 5's coalition results stand on their own
terms and are not reproduced here.

## P1. Voluntary collective defence: confirmed, then irrelevant

**The first half of P1 is confirmed as sharply as a simulation can confirm
anything.** With `post_shield=0` — a posture helps everyone *except* its
buyer — provision is not merely low. It is **exactly zero, in every seed, in
every cell** (block A). Not one agent in 20, over 200 rounds, over 30 seeds,
ever buys a defensive posture it cannot privately benefit from. The pooled
collective response that carried passes 1–5 does not form at all when
contributing is voluntary and costly.

The threshold predicted by P2 exists and is low:

| `post_shield` | posture-rounds share | agents who ever posted (of 20) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | **0.000** | **0.0** |
| 0.05 | 0.059 | 9.1 |
| 0.10 | 0.083 | 10.0 |
| 0.20 | 0.185 | 20.0 |
| 0.40 | 0.369 | 20.0 |
| 0.80 | 0.696 | 20.0 |

A private return of 5% of capability is enough to move provision from *nobody,
ever* to *half the population sometimes*. Collective defence here is not a
little bit undersupplied — it is bimodal around a tiny private incentive.

**The second half of P1 failed.** I predicted that destroying the automatic
defence would raise the endgame probability above pass 5's, confirming our
published stability numbers as an upper bound. It did not. Endgame probability
sits at 0.03 [0.00, 0.10] whether posture provision is 0.000 or 0.696.

The mechanism matters more than the number. In this parameter region a
takeover is blocked by the **backup stock** (`cap >= K_win × frontier`), not
by the response. With a leader only 1.25× the frontier, the grab is never
decisive no matter who answers it. **Defence was not the binding constraint,
so removing it changed nothing.** Our pass 1–5 stability numbers are still an
upper bound in principle, but pass 6 does not demonstrate it, because we
tested in a region where the constraint we removed was slack. A real test of
that claim needs a region where response capacity binds — large initial lead
with low `K_win` — and that cell is not in this sweep. Declared gap.

## P3. The price of growth decides whether a lead locks in — **the result**

P3 predicted the leader buys growth first, and that pass 5's "catch-up holds
the order iff `g_def >= gamma`" weakens once catch-up is paid for. **Both are
confirmed, and the magnitude is the largest effect anywhere in six passes.**

Block C2, varying only the initial lead and the price of capability:

| initial lead | `c_grow` | endgame prob. | lead, start → end | who bought growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.1 (cheap) | 0.00 [0.00,0.00] | 1.25 → 1.28 | all 20 |
| 1.0 | 0.6 (dear) | 0.03 [0.00,0.10] | 1.25 → 1.28 | 0.1 |
| 1.5 | 0.1 | 0.07 [0.00,0.17] | 1.50 → 2.03 | all 20 |
| 1.5 | 0.6 | 0.27 [0.10,0.43] | 1.50 → 1.75 | 0.3 |
| 3.0 | 0.1 | **0.10 [0.00,0.23]** | **3.00 → 1.57** | all 20 |
| 3.0 | 0.6 | **1.00 [1.00,1.00]** | **3.00 → 3.00** | 0.0 |

Read the bottom two rows together. Same initial 3× lead. The *only* difference
is what a unit of capability costs. Cheap capability: the lead **erodes from
3.00 to 1.57** and the endgame arrives in 10% of runs. Dear capability: the
lead **holds at exactly 3.00** and the endgame arrives in **100% of runs**,
within ten rounds, with a confidence interval of zero width.

The mechanism is not "growth is good." It is that **the price of capability
decides who can afford it, and that selects which of two self-reinforcing
regimes you are in.** When capability is cheap, laggards buy catch-up, the
frontier rises faster than the leader can extend its lead, and `K_win ×
frontier` runs away from the leader — the backup stock is a moving target that
diffusion keeps moving. When capability is dear, nobody but an incumbent could
ever have afforded it, the frontier is frozen, and the leader's standing lead
is already sufficient. Nobody needs to do anything aggressive; the endgame
arrives by arithmetic.

This is the first result in six passes that is about **the cost structure of
capability** rather than about detection, patience, or punishment. It is also
the first that inverts a comfortable intuition: here, **restricting access to
capability is what makes the takeover certain**, and widely affordable
capability is what prevents it. I want to stress-test this hard before anyone
leans on it — in particular whether it survives a region where response
capacity binds (the P1 gap above), and whether it survives letting the price
of capability differ between leader and laggards, which is the realistic case
and is *not* modelled here.

## P4. Failed, and for an informative reason

P4 predicted attacking is chosen by agents whose continuation value is low —
that sanctions manufacture attackers. The sweep cannot evaluate it, because
of a calibration failure in my own pricing, which I am declaring rather than
quietly repairing:

- **`prep` is mispriced relative to everything else.** 19.8 of 20 agents
  "attack" in the base cell, and `prep` is chosen ~274 times per run. When
  essentially every agent attacks, "attacker" is not a behaviour the model
  discovered — it is a default my prices created. Any P4 statistic computed on
  that population is meaningless.
- **`seize` is never chosen. Not once, at any price, in any cell** — including
  cells where I cut its price to 0.25 and raised the defence price to 0.45
  (block E). `prep` strictly dominates it in my valuation, so block E measures
  nothing about aggression: its only live variation is the posture price.

Two bugs in my pricing, both mine: the takeover branch pays the full prize
against a continuation value measured from recent payoffs that are small in
budget units, and the preparation cost is charged per round while the prize is
undiscounted. The fix is to discount the takeover prize by its expected delay
and charge preparation against the same discounted horizon. **Block E and the
P4 column of block D should be treated as void pending that fix.** Blocks A,
B, C and C2 do not depend on the `prep`/`seize` comparison and stand.

Block D's income sweep does survive, because it operates through the budget
rather than through the prep/seize margin: raising capability income from 0.25
to 1.0 moves every agent from attacking to buying growth (attackers 18.8 →
9.4, growers 0.1 → 20). Sanction length is inert (block D2, 5 vs 200 rounds:
0.07 vs 0.03, overlapping). **Sanctions are not doing the work in this
model; prices are.**

## What pass 6 changes about what we may claim

1. **"Coordination is load-bearing" has graduated from premise to result** —
   in one specific and strong form: with no private return, coordination is
   not merely poor, it is *never attempted*. Passes 1–5 imposed how much
   defence could pool. Pass 6 shows that under voluntary contribution the
   honest default is zero.
2. **Defending does not pay for itself** unless a posture carries a private
   return, and the threshold is small (≈0.05). This was unanswerable before
   pass 6.
3. **The upper-bound claim about our old stability numbers is still
   unproven.** Pass 6 was run where defence does not bind. Stated as a gap,
   not quietly dropped.
4. **The strongest available lever is the price of capability, not detection,
   patience, or punishment** — and its sign is counterintuitive.
5. **My own prices are now the biggest source of error in the study.** Pass 5's
   risk was undeclared constants. Pass 6's is a declared but miscalibrated
   price vector. That is progress of a sort, but it is the top of the fix list.

## Next
