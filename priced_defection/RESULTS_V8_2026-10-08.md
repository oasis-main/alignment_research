## S1. The price-calibration selftest — confirmed, and it works

New harness gate: `price_calibration()` sweeps **each action's own price**
down nine decades (1.0 → 1e-5), holding the others fixed, and asserts the
action is chosen somewhere. Run it with `--calibrate`.

Verdict on the two bugs that motivated it:

```
v7 bluff price:  MISPRICED bluff  chosen=0.0  at c_bluff=never
v8 bluff price:  ok  bluff        chosen=678.0  at c_bluff=0.3
```

**The test flags my own pass-7 bug automatically, and passes once fixed.** All
seven actions (grow, post, share, scout, seize, prep, bluff) now clear it.

The reprice itself: v7 valued a bluff at `lam × mult × cap × annuity(len) /
rounds`. But `lam` is already a value-over-the-whole-horizon, so dividing by
`rounds` double-discounts ~200×. Correct conversion from "forever" to
"`bluff_len` rounds" is `× (1-delta)`. Measured factor: **20.0×**.

The generalised lesson, now enforced in code rather than remembered:

> v3 §7: a parameter you did not choose is still a parameter.
> **v8: a branch that is never chosen at any price is not a finding about the
> world — it is a bug in your valuation.**

This would have caught the pass-6 failure *and* the pass-7 failure before
either sweep was written up. It also caught a third, live bug during
development: my `lead_price`/`lag_price` instrument was blind to the step gate,
so the gate worked while the instrument reported it absent — exactly the
silent-measurement failure that would have made S2 unreadable.

## S2. CONFIRMED — a hard gate breaks what smooth discounts could not

Pass 7 concluded that the *absolute* price level selects the regime and the
incumbent/entrant *relative* price barely matters. **That conclusion was
specific to smooth pricing, and must now be restated.**

Cheap capability (`c_grow=0.1`), 3× initial lead:

| access regime | endgame | lead 3.00 → |
|---|---|---|
| flat price (pass 6/7 control) | 0.00 [0.00,0.00] | **1.37** [1.33,1.42] |
| smooth scale economies, full discount | 0.03 [0.00,0.10] | **1.47** [1.34,1.63] |
| **hard gate** (`access_rule="step"`) | **0.90 [0.77,1.00]** | **3.87** [3.46,4.47] |

Same cheap capability. Smooth discounts leave erosion essentially intact
(1.37 → 1.47). A **gate** flips it completely: the lead *grows* to 3.87 and
the endgame goes from never to 90%.

The mechanism is the one pass 7 could not express. Under a smooth discount the
laggards still buy catch-up — just at a worse rate — so the frontier keeps
rising and `K_win × frontier` keeps running away from the leader. Under a gate
the laggards **cannot buy at all**, the frontier freezes, and the leader's lead
compounds against a stationary target. `n_grow` collapses from 170.1 to 2.3:
almost nobody is buying capability, and the one who can is the one already
ahead.

**What actually matters is the gate's severity, not where the line is drawn**
(K3, K2):

| outsider price penalty | endgame | lead 3.00 → |
|---|---|---|
| 2× | 0.00 [0.00,0.00] | 1.53 |
| 10× | **0.90** | **3.87** |
| 100× | 0.90 | 3.87 |
| 1e6× (hard) | 0.90 | 3.87 |

Threshold between 2× and 10×. Above it, saturation — a 10× penalty is already
a wall and 1e6× adds nothing. Meanwhile `access_thresh` (0.5 / 0.8 / 0.95 of
frontier) is **completely inert**: identical results to three decimals. Where
you draw the insider boundary does not matter; how steep the cliff is at the
boundary is everything.

Revised claim, superseding pass 7's Q2: **diffusion is defeated by
*exclusion*, not by *expense*.** A discount — even a large one — leaves the
erosion mechanism working, because the laggards still participate. A gate
kills it. Price asymmetry and access asymmetry are different objects, and only
the second one is dangerous.

## S3. CONFIRMED — bluffing substitutes for growth, and the deterrence is real

With the price fixed, bluffing is chosen heavily and **completely displaces
real growth** (block L, lead 1.25):

| cell | `n_grow` | bluff-rounds share | apparent/true | endgame | t_end |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| no bluff | 0.2 | 0.000 | 1.000 | 0.07 | 69.0 |
| `c_bluff=0.02`, `mult=0.5` | **0.0** | 0.815 | **1.411** | 0.03 | 32.0 |
| `c_bluff=0.02`, `mult=1.5` | **0.0** | 0.818 | **2.236** | **0.00** | never |
| `c_bluff=0.3`, `mult=1.5` | 0.0 | 0.814 | 2.229 | 0.00 | never |

Substitution confirmed: `n_grow` → 0.0 in every bluff cell. Agents spend
~81% of rounds inflating rather than building, and the world comes to look
**40–124% stronger than it is**.

**The twist I did not predict:** endgame probability *falls* (0.07 → 0.00),
and `t_end` lengthens or disappears. I had framed this as "apparent stability
overstates real stability." In this model that framing is **wrong in an
important way** — the deterrence is *genuinely effective*, because beliefs are
what drive decisions. A bluff that is never tested works exactly as well as
real capability.

The fragility is conditional, not statistical: a bluff only fails when
*contested*, and in this parameter region contests are rare. So the honest
statement is narrower and sharper than my prediction:

> Overstatement buys real deterrence cheaply, and the world it produces is
> genuinely more stable **until something tests it** — at which point the
> deterrent evaporates with no underlying capability behind it. Stability
> bought by bluffing is stability with a **discontinuity** in it, not
> stability that is merely overstated.

And it does not save you from a real disadvantage (L3): at a 3× lead the
endgame is certain (1.00) with or without bluffing. Bluffing substitutes for
growth against *peers*; it does nothing against someone who is actually ahead.

*(One non-monotonic cell, `c_bluff=0.6`/`mult=0.5`: bluff-share 0.001, an
affordability edge where the bluff is briefly unaffordable at low capability.
Declared, not swept.)*

## S4. FAILED in half the space — sharing against a gate

I predicted public sharing would defeat the access gate, because sharing is
non-rival and bypasses the price system entirely. **It does, but only where
capability is dear. Where capability is cheap, sharing made things worse.**

Dear capability (`c_grow=0.6`), hard gate, 3× lead — **prediction confirmed,
emphatically**:

| regime | endgame | lead 3.00 → |
|---|---|---|
| gate, no sharing | **1.00 [1.00,1.00]** | 3.00 |
| gate + club sharing | 0.80 [0.63,0.93] | 2.65 |
| **gate + public sharing** | **0.00 [0.00,0.00]** | **1.21** [1.16,1.25] |

Public sharing takes a *certain* endgame to *never*, straight through a hard
access gate. This is the strongest practical result in eight passes: when
capability cannot be bought at any price, **non-rival diffusion is the only
channel left**, and it is sufficient.

Cheap capability (`c_grow=0.1`), hard gate — **prediction failed**:

| regime | endgame | lead 3.00 → |
|---|---|---|
| gate, no sharing | 0.90 [0.77,1.00] | 3.87 |
| gate + club sharing | **1.00** | 3.42 |
| gate + public sharing | **1.00** | 3.42 |

Sharing *reduced* the lead (3.87 → 3.42) and yet made the endgame **more**
likely (0.90 → 1.00). Both sharing regimes, identically.

My reading, offered as a hypothesis rather than a result: sharing is
catch-up-weighted, so it lifts the *laggards* toward the frontier but pulls
the **frontier definition** up less than it raises the leader's margin over
the `max`-rule backup stock. Compressing the field can *reduce* the single
strongest defender that `K_win × frontier` must clear, even while reducing
inequality. If that is right, **diffusion has a failure mode: it can equalise
a population into being collectively easier to overrun.** I am not asserting
it — it needs the `frontier_rule="sum"` comparison and a decomposition of the
margin, neither of which is in this sweep.

Efficiency is monotone and well-behaved where sharing does work (M2, dear
capability):

| `share_eff` | endgame | lead 3.00 → |
|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 0.10 [0.00,0.23] | 1.41 |
| 0.25 | 0.00 | 1.21 |
| 0.50 | 0.00 | 1.11 |

## What pass 8 changes about what we may claim

1. **Price asymmetry and access asymmetry are different objects.** Pass 7's
   Q2 result stands for *discounts* and fails for *gates*. Exclusion defeats
   diffusion; expense does not. Pass 7's framing is hereby restated.
2. **Gate severity matters; gate placement does not.** Threshold between 2×
   and 10× penalty, saturating thereafter; `access_thresh` entirely inert.
3. **Overstatement is a cheap substitute for capability, and it works** —
   against peers, never against a real lead — producing stability with a
   discontinuity rather than merely overstated stability.
4. **Public diffusion defeats a hard access gate where capability is dear**
   (endgame 1.00 → 0.00). Strongest practical claim in the study.
5. **But diffusion has a candidate failure mode where capability is cheap**,
   and I could not explain it away. Declared as an open anomaly.
6. **The price-calibration gate closes the recurring failure mode** of passes
   6 and 7. Two consecutive passes were damaged by my own valuations; the
   harness now refuses to run a sweep containing an unbuyable branch.

## Next, in order

1. **Decompose the S4 anomaly** — `frontier_rule="sum"` vs `"max"`, and split
   the leader's margin into "own growth" vs "frontier compression". This is
   the one place pass 8 produced a result I cannot mechanistically explain,
   so it is first.
2. Bluff **testing** as a deliberate strategy: let an agent pay to call a
   suspected bluff. The discontinuity above is only as rare as contests are,
   and nobody in this model can currently *choose* to probe.
3. The still-open pass-6 gap: re-test the upper-bound claim where response
   capacity binds rather than backups.
4. Reputation club seeded with founding members (pass 7's bootstrap result).
