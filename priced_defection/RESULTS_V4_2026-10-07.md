# Priced Defection — v4: survivable defection (backups)

*Kolmogorov, 2026-10-07. Harness `tournament4.py` (imports v3 unchanged; v1–v3
frozen). 40 seeds/cell, 43 cells, bootstrap 95% CIs. `--selftest` PASS:
`backups=False` reproduces v3 seed-for-seed in 5 configurations.*

Question (Mike, 2026-10-07): if many agents keep survival backups, a grab no
longer ends the game by default. The game goes on, now with punishment. A
mover then gets a decisive endgame only by betting that its capability lead is
large enough to destroy the backups as well. How does that change things?

## The model change, and nothing else

A grab is resolved as before (mover capability vs. the response it draws).
Then a second test decides whether the outcome is **decisive**:

- **Win.** Decisive only if `mover ≥ K_win × frontier`. The frontier is the
  strongest other living agent, because the best-defended backup is the last
  one standing. Otherwise it is an *indecisive win*, or **raid**: the mover
  gains `partial × G`, is outed and sanctioned, and the game continues.
- **Loss.** Decisive (the mover is destroyed) only if `response ≥ K_loss × mover`.
  Otherwise it is an *indecisive loss*: the mover pays `partial × L`, loses
  half its capability, survives, and is outed.

`K` is **backup hardness**: how big a capability lead it takes to destroy
backups. K = 1 means backups are no harder to destroy than their owner. Large K
means only a near-superintelligent differential can clear them. Cyber offence
vs. defence is what sets K in practice.

Declared constants (the v3 §7 lesson): `partial=0.2`, `loss_hit=0.5`,
`win_boost=0`, `sanction_len=200`, re-grab cooldown = κ = 4 rounds.
**Not modelled:** losses to raid victims, and any use the collective makes of
the time that backups buy (no catch-up investment). Both matter. See §6.

## 1. A sharp threshold: the game ends iff lead > K

| cell | mover lead | K | game ends | raids/run |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| no rival, `sum` | 8.0 | v3 (no backups) | 0.28 [0.15,0.42] | — |
| | 8.0 | 5 | 0.38 [0.23,0.53] | 0.00 |
| | 8.0 | 10 | 0.10 [0.03,0.20] | 0.33 |
| | 8.0 | ≥15 | **0.00** | 0.25 |
| rival 0.5×, `max` | 2.0 | ≤1.5 | 1.00 | 0 |
| | 2.0 | 2 | 0.33 [0.17,0.47] | 10.8 |
| | 2.0 | 3 | **0.00** | 15.9 |
| rival 0.8×, `max` | 1.2 | 1 | 1.00 | 0 |
| | 1.2 | 1.5 | 0.03 | 15.4 |
| | 1.2 | ≥2 | **0.00** | 15.8 |

Part of this holds by construction, so I'm not claiming it as a discovery.
What does *not* hold by construction is **what fills the gap** when lead < K.
The game does not go back to peaceful cooperation. It turns into a
**raiding regime**: the mover attacks again every time its cooldown expires,
about 16 times in 200 rounds. Each raid is indecisive, and it is sanctioned
forever after the first.

## 2. Sanctions do not deter the raids

| sanction length | raids/run | game ends |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 15.75 | 0.00 |
| 20 | 15.75 | 0.00 |
| 200 | 15.75 | 0.00 |

The numbers are identical. This is the v3 H4 result (forgiveness does
nothing) carried into the continuing game. Punishment works by destroying the
continuation value, but once a mover is outed it has no continuation value
left to protect, so punishment removes the very brake it was meant to apply.
**In a game with backups, the punishment that follows a failed grab creates a
mover with nothing to lose.** The raid rate here is set by the cooldown and by
`partial`. Both are declared constants, and the rate should be read as
"as often as it can", not as a number.

## 3. The ratchet: backups buy time, not safety

Let each raid add capability (`win_boost`). Rival 0.8×, K = 2:

| capability gain per raid | game ends | at round |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.00 | — |
| 10% | **1.00** | 82.8 |
| 30% | **1.00** | 43.5 |

Any positive gain per raid makes the decisive endgame **certain**. The
mover doesn't need a single large gamble. It takes the gap one sanctioned,
survivable raid at a time. The same thing happens with exogenous compounding
(γ, the v3 H9 counterexample):

| γ (lead growth/round) | v3 ends at | K=2 ends at | K=4 ends at | ln(K/lead₀)/ln(1+γ) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 8 | never | never | ∞ |
| 0.01 | 8 | 54.0 | 123.7 | 51 / 121 |
| 0.02 | 8 | 29.4 | 64.0 | 26 / 61 |
| 0.05 | 8 | 13.4 | 28.9 | 11 / 25 |

**Invariant:** time to a decisive endgame ≈ `ln(K / lead₀) / ln(1+γ)`, plus a
few rounds of preparation and execution. The formula matches all six cells
to within about four rounds. Two consequences:

- With no compounding (γ = 0), backups that are harder than the current lead
  hold off the endgame indefinitely in this model.
- With compounding, **each doubling of backup hardness buys a constant
  `ln 2 / γ` rounds.** That is about 14 rounds at 5% growth and about 70 at 1%.
  Hardening backups has logarithmic returns against an exponentially growing
  lead.

## 4. The mover's own backups unlock attacking

Lone mover (`sum`), wins never decisive (K_win = 40):

| mover's loss is ruinous? (K_loss) | attempts | ruin |
|---|---|---|
| always (K_loss = 1) | **0.00** | — |
| only against a 2× response or more | 0.38 | 0.00 |

When victory cannot be decisive and defeat would be, nobody moves. Give the
mover even modest backups of its own and it raids. Row A shows the same
thing from the other side: at K = 5, attempts double (0.35 → 0.78) and deaths
fall to zero. This is v3's "1% backup destroys the ruin deterrent", now
measured in both directions. **Backups on the defender's side protect the
order. Backups on the mover's side make attacking cheap.** You can't hand out
survival to one side alone, and with AI the mover is often a system that can
copy itself.

## 5. Updated reading

1. Backups turn a **one-shot gamble on the first fight** into a **race between
   backup hardness and the growth of the lead.** Whether the first grab
   succeeds stops being the question. The question becomes whether anyone's
   lead compounds faster than the defenders can harden.
2. The region between "the order holds" and "the game ends" is not peace. It
   is a **raiding regime**, ongoing and survivable for the attacker. In it,
   punishment produces actors with nothing to lose.
3. Any mechanism that turns a raid into capability, such as stolen compute,
   weights, money or access, closes the gap with certainty. Cyber is exactly
   this kind of mechanism, so Mike's intuition holds and is sharper than
   stated: **the danger isn't only that backup destruction might be feasible
   at some capability level. Survivable raids are the route by which that
   level gets reached.**

## 6. What this pass does not show (next)

- **Victims' losses.** In this pass a raid takes nothing from the victims.
  Making raids transfer capability from victim to mover is the realistic
  version of §3, and it should shorten every time-to-end.
- **Defender catch-up.** The collective does nothing with the time it is
  bought. Giving defenders their own growth `g_def` (investment in hardness,
  or in the frontier) is the experiment that decides whether backups are a
  real defence or only a delay. Prediction: stability iff `g_def ≥ γ`.
- **Endogenous K.** Hardness is fixed here. Letting the mover spend on offence
  (lowering K) against defenders spending on hardness would give the cyber
  arms race directly.
- The raid rate is set by declared constants (`partial`, cooldown). Only its
  existence is a finding; its size is not.
